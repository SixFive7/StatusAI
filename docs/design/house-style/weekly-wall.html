⚠ Style template, not a specification

Everything this page describes was reverted. Its premise is false.

This file is in the repository for one reason: it is the house style for visual documentation of the status line. Copy its presentation: the character-cell terminal rendering, the palette lifted verbatim from Program.cs, the before/after panes, the column-anchored annotations and the habit of stating what a design costs in characters. Do not copy, implement or reason from its content.

  • The claim was that the 5-hour session limit resets when the 7-day weekly limit resets, so the 5h projection should stop at the weekly boundary.
  • That is false. The session window is independent: session.resets_at follows its own schedule, and the weekly reset does not touch it.
  • The change was built, deployed and reverted. src/Program.cs carries none of it: no BarCut, no WallColor, no walled, no projFull. The ⇥ and ┃ marks shown below do not exist in the product, and will never be added on this reasoning.

The correct limit model is in docs/reference/limits.md, and the full record of why this was rejected in docs/design/rejected-designs.md.

The Weekly Wall

As originally written, this page argued that the 7d window resets at 05:00, 2h30m before the 5h window's own reset at 07:30, and takes the 5h counter with it, so the session projection has to stop there. That reasoning does not hold. Everything below is preserved as written, at the live numbers from 03:49, purely as a worked example of the documentation format.

The two marks

⇥ 1h11m The wall column

On a walled row the "time to 100%" field is meaningless, because you never get there. It is spent on the wall instead: → becomes ⇥ and the value becomes the 7d countdown. Both clocks stay on the row: the row's own reset at ↻ 3h41m, and the one that actually governs at ⇥ 1h11m. It costs zero characters, since → 1h54m and ⇥ 1h11m are the same seven cells.

●●┃●✗✗ The cut in the bar

The projection bar keeps the full 161% trajectory, but ┃ cuts it at the wall. Everything left of the cut is where you actually land: 75%. Everything right of it keeps its shape, including the six ✗ of overshoot, but is ghosted, because that is the part the week cancels. The bar grows from 16 cells to 17 and the percent column shrinks from 3 to 2, so the row width is unchanged. The cut is drawn only where something is actually cut, never on a row that was going to end at the wall anyway.

Only the session row is walled

RowKindAgainst the wall
5h Session. A rolling five-hour window whose reset drifts with use: 07:30 today, 2h30m the far side of the weekly boundary. It is the only row that can run into the wall. ⇥ 1h11m and a cut bar
7d Weekly. It is the wall. Untouched
Fable Weekly, model-scoped. Resets with the wall rather than into it: 04:59:59.821391 against 04:59:59.821140. Untouched

In code this is one field. Wall is infinite for both weekly rows, so walled is false and every branch the change adds stays dormant for them. It stays dormant for the session row too for most of the week, until the weekly reset finally overtakes it.

Colour rule

Both marks take the 7d row's severity colour rather than a fixed one: amber today, because weekly_all reports severity: "warning" at 81%, green while it is normal and red once critical. So the wall tells you both when the thing that governs you resets and how much trouble it is in. It reuses SevColor, which is already in the file. If you would rather it stayed one fixed colour, that is a one-line change.

Outcome: the premise did not hold

The caveat this page shipped with turned out to be the whole story. Nothing in the OAuth payload ever confirmed the coupling: session.resets_at stayed at 07:30 regardless of the weekly boundary, and the array marked session: is_active=false against weekly_all: is_active=true. The design rested on an observation that was later withdrawn. It was reverted in full: the source was restored and hash-verified against the pre-change file, the binary rebuilt from it, and both wall glyphs confirmed absent from the rendered output.

What survives is the presentation, and one habit that applies well beyond this feature: design a display so that a bad assumption stays visible instead of silently disappearing. The ghosted-tail bar was chosen because it kept the un-walled 161% trajectory on screen instead of deleting it, and of the five options it was the only one that would have degraded gracefully if the premise were wrong, as it turned out to be.
